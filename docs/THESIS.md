# The thesis

Every useful system exposes some way to observe it, influence it, or exchange information with it. A neural network accepts tensors. A fridge exposes temperatures and controls. A dashboard exposes views, filters, and actions. Their implementations differ, but cooperation begins with the same questions: what can I observe, what can I request, what will happen, and am I allowed to do it?

**Maiku’s thesis is that these interactions can share a common operational language while retaining their native meaning.** A model remains a model, an appliance remains an appliance, and a dashboard remains a dashboard. Maiku supplies the connective layer through which another system can discover their capabilities, exchange compatible inputs and outputs, and exercise permitted actions.

Today, integration requires people to translate between interfaces repeatedly. Each connection embeds assumptions about formats, authentication, timing, state, and consequences. As systems multiply and change, this translation becomes expensive and fragile. A universal adapter would make those assumptions explicit, reusable, and testable. Its fundamental unit would be an interaction contract: an account of an operation, its requirements, its observable outcomes, and its limits.

Universality therefore requires two complementary mechanisms. First, a small extensible contract must accommodate arbitrary systems with accessible interfaces. Second, an adaptation process must discover those interfaces and construct bindings with progressively less manual work. Existing descriptions, source code, signatures, device protocols, documentation, and visible interfaces become evidence from which Maiku proposes an adapter. Human guidance fills gaps that available evidence cannot resolve.

The central difficulty is meaning. Matching input shapes does not establish compatibility. Two models may accept identical tensors while requiring different normalization. Two controls labelled “reset” may have radically different consequences. Maiku must represent uncertainty, preserve contextual requirements, and distinguish declared behavior from observed behavior. Adaptation succeeds when the resulting contract supports correct interaction within stated conditions.

**Verification must therefore be part of adaptation itself.** Maiku should test permitted operations, validate outputs, detect interface drift, and retain evidence supporting each capability. Verification remains bounded: finite tests cannot establish every future behavior, and observation cannot reveal every hidden consequence. The adapter should expose those limits and require additional evidence or human judgment when an operation exceeds them.

This creates a practical architecture: discover, describe, bind, verify, execute, and maintain. The execution layer handles authority, state, events, artifacts, failures, and human decisions consistently. Specialized connectors supply access to particular environments. Learned methods can propose mappings and repairs, while explicit checks constrain what those proposals may do. Every successful adaptation can contribute reusable knowledge without exposing private user data.

The public benefit is making existing capabilities accessible across technical and economic boundaries. People could combine local models, household devices, institutional software, and personal tools without rebuilding every connection or depending on one vendor. The project’s enduring value would accumulate through trustworthy adaptations, shared tests, and community maintenance. Its research question is precise: how much of integration can be automated while preserving meaning, authority, and observable correctness? Its ultimate ambition is equally clear: make any accessible system a dependable participant in a larger world of cooperating systems, while keeping people in control throughout.
